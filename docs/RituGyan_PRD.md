# Product Requirements Document (PRD)

## RituGyan — Regime-Aware AI Post-Processing of Monsoon Rainfall Forecasts

**Document version:** 1.0
**Status:** Draft for Review
**Prepared for:** Problem Statement Submission — AI/ML Rainfall Forecasting

---

## 1. Executive Summary

RituGyan (ऋतु + ज्ञान — "Season Knowledge") is an AI/ML-based rainfall post-processing system that improves the accuracy of monsoon rainfall forecasts by first identifying the prevailing **weather regime** (active monsoon, break monsoon, depression/low, orographic, coastal, western disturbance) and then applying a **correction method tailored to that regime**, rather than a single generic correction applied uniformly to all conditions.

The system outputs a corrected, district-level rainfall forecast along with a **heavy rainfall exceedance probability**, and is accompanied by a transparent verification report scoring performance against the raw NWP (Numerical Weather Prediction) baseline.

---

## 2. Problem Statement

### 2.1 The Core Problem

Raw rainfall forecasts generated by NWP models (e.g., GFS, WRF, IMD's own models) contain **systematic biases that vary by weather regime**:

| Regime | Typical NWP Error Pattern |
|---|---|
| Active Monsoon | Often over-predicts widespread rainfall extent/intensity |
| Break Monsoon | Struggles to capture the reduced-rainfall lull accurately |
| Monsoon Depression/Low | Under-predicts intensity, smears rainfall over too wide an area |
| Orographic (mountain-driven) | Poor resolution of terrain-triggered rainfall (e.g., Western Ghats) |
| Coastal | Land–sea contrast errors distort rainfall gradients |
| Western Disturbance | Different physical drivers than monsoon systems; frequently mis-modeled |

Because these error patterns differ, **a single bias-correction formula applied uniformly across all regimes cannot perform well everywhere**. It will over-correct some situations and under-correct others — and the situations where it fails most often are exactly the **heavy and very heavy rainfall events** that matter most operationally (floods, disaster response, agriculture).

### 2.2 Why This Matters Now

- Heavy rainfall events are rare in frequency but disproportionately high in impact (floods, crop damage, infrastructure disruption).
- Standard skill-score optimization (e.g., overall RMSE) is dominated by the much more frequent light/moderate rainfall cases, meaning **extreme event accuracy gets statistically "averaged away"** — even though it is the most operationally critical.
- India's forecasting needs are increasingly **hyperlocal** (district/grid-level), while most correction methods still operate at coarse regional scales.

## 4. Market Gap — What Currently Exists vs. What's Missing

### 4.1 Current State of the Market

- Most operational bias-correction (where deployed) uses **static, global statistical methods** — e.g., simple quantile mapping or model output statistics (MOS) — applied the same way regardless of the meteorological situation.
- **Regime identification is a manual, expert-driven process.** Experienced forecasters recognize "this looks like a break monsoon" or "this is a depression" through training and pattern recognition — this knowledge is rarely systematized into an automated pipeline.
- Verification/scoring of forecast improvements is typically reported as a **single blended skill score**, which obscures regime-specific performance — a correction method might look "good on average" while performing poorly during the exact conditions (depressions, orographic events) that cause the most damage.
- Existing rainfall products emphasize **deterministic values** ("expect 60mm") rather than **probabilistic, threshold-based risk communication** ("72% chance of exceeding very heavy rainfall"), which is less actionable for disaster response.
- District/grid-level granularity, when available, is often decoupled from any regime-aware correction — the two capabilities exist separately, not as one integrated pipeline.

### 4.2 The Gap RituGyan Fills

| Gap | RituGyan's Response |
|---|---|
| No automated regime detection | Purpose-built classifier trained on NWP fields, synoptic indices, and historical regime-defining patterns |
| One-size-fits-all correction | Regime-conditional correction — separate/adaptive correction behavior per regime |
| Deterministic-only output | Adds heavy rainfall exceedance probability against IMD operational thresholds |
| Blended, opaque verification | Regime-stratified verification (RMSE, ETS, CSI, POD, FAR, FSS reported per regime) |
| Coarse regional forecasts | District/grid-level output, decision-ready for local stakeholders |
| Expert-dependent regime knowledge | Encodes forecaster-level pattern recognition into a repeatable, scalable AI pipeline |

**In one sentence:** RituGyan fills the gap between *"physics-based raw forecasts"* and *"actionable, trustworthy, locally-relevant rainfall risk information"* — by making the correction step aware of *what kind of weather is actually happening*, rather than blind to it.

## 5. Proposed Solution

### 5.1 System Overview

A two-stage AI pipeline, plus a verification layer:

```
Raw NWP Forecast
      │
      ▼
[Stage 1] Weather Regime Classifier
      │   → Classifies: Active / Break / Depression / Orographic / Coastal / Western Disturbance
      ▼
[Stage 2] Regime-Conditional Bias Correction
      │   → Applies a correction model/function tailored to the detected regime
      ▼
Outputs:
  • Bias-corrected district/grid-level rainfall forecast
  • Heavy rainfall exceedance probability (vs. operational thresholds)
  • District-level rainfall product (table/map)

[Continuous] Verification Layer
      → Compares corrected vs. raw forecast using RMSE, ETS, CSI, POD, FAR, FSS
      → Reported regime-by-regime, not blended
```

### 5.2 Stage 1 — Weather Regime Classifier

- **Inputs:** NWP model fields (wind, moisture, pressure, OLR), synoptic indices (e.g., monsoon onset/break indices), satellite indicators.
- **Approach:** Supervised ML classifier (e.g., gradient boosting, random forest, or CNN/LSTM for spatial-temporal fields), trained on historically labeled regime periods.
- **Output:** A regime label for the current forecast period (and ideally a confidence score).

### 5.3 Stage 2 — Regime-Conditional Bias Correction

- **Approach options:**
  - Separate quantile-mapping correction functions trained per regime, or
  - A single ML correction model (e.g., gradient-boosted trees / neural network) with regime as an explicit categorical input feature.
- **Output:** Corrected rainfall value at district/grid level, plus probability of exceeding IMD-defined heavy/very-heavy rainfall thresholds (e.g., 64.5mm, 115.6mm, 204.5mm/day).

### 5.4 Verification Layer

Standard forecast verification metrics, computed **per regime** rather than as one blended score:

| Metric | What It Measures |
|---|---|
| RMSE | Overall magnitude error |
| ETS (Equitable Threat Score) | Skill at predicting threshold-exceeding events, adjusted for chance |
| CSI (Critical Success Index) | Hit rate accounting for misses and false alarms |
| POD (Probability of Detection) | How many real heavy-rain events were correctly caught |
| FAR (False Alarm Ratio) | How many predicted events did not occur |
| FSS (Fraction Skill Score) | Spatial skill, tolerant of small location displacement |

## 7. Innovation Summary

1. **Context-aware correction, not static correction.** The correction step is conditioned on *what kind of weather is happening*, rather than applying the same formula universally.
2. **Optimized for the tail, not the average.** Deliberately targets heavy/very-heavy rainfall skill — the highest-impact, hardest-to-forecast cases — rather than optimizing for overall average-case error, which is dominated by common light-rain days.
3. **Automates expert judgment.** Encodes the pattern-recognition currently held only by experienced human forecasters into a repeatable, scalable pipeline.
4. **Probabilistic, decision-ready output.** Moves from "how much rain" to "how likely is this to be dangerous" — closer to how cyclone/earthquake risk communication works.
5. **Transparent, regime-wise verification.** Builds forecaster trust by showing exactly where and how much the correction helps, rather than hiding performance behind one blended score.

## 9. Assumptions & Constraints

- Historical NWP forecast data and observed rainfall data (district/grid-level) are available for model training and verification.
- Regime labels for historical training data are derived from established IMD definitions/indices (monsoon onset/break criteria, depression tracks, etc.).
- Initial scope is monsoon-season regimes over India; framework is designed to be extensible to other seasons (e.g., winter western disturbances) and regions.
- Operational thresholds for "heavy" and "very heavy" rainfall follow IMD's standard definitions.

---

## 10. Future Scope

- Extend regime classification to additional seasons beyond monsoon (e.g., pre-monsoon thunderstorm regimes, post-monsoon cyclone-influenced rainfall).
- Incorporate ensemble NWP inputs (multiple model runs) to improve both classification and correction robustness.
- Real-time operational dashboard integration for forecaster use.
- Expand probabilistic output beyond binary threshold exceedance to full probability distributions.
